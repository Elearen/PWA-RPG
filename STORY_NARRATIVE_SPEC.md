STORY & NARRATIVE SYSTEM SPECIFICATION
Document: STORY_AND_NARRATIVE_SPEC Version: 0.2 Status: Active Design Purpose: Defines the narrative structure, world design, three protagonist starting storylines, journal-based storytelling, exploration, story progression and integration with the RPG systems.
 
⸻
 
1. NARRATIVE DESIGN PHILOSOPHY
The game is fundamentally a:
Story-driven RPG with automated/idle combat.
The automated combat system must not make the narrative feel secondary.
The story should instead be experienced through:
exploration
discovery
journeys
locations
NPCs
points of interest
encounters
journal entries
environmental/world information
major story events
The player is not expected to repeatedly stop and read long conventional dialogue scenes.
The narrative is delivered in small, digestible story bites that gradually build the player’s understanding of the world.
 
⸻
 
2. NARRATIVE STRUCTURE
The game uses a hybrid structure:
Linear major narrative + free exploration within unlocked regions.
The major story is coherent and shared.
The player cannot accidentally miss a required major story beat and thereby make the main narrative impossible to complete.
However, the player is not given a rigid checklist telling them exactly:
“Go to Location X, speak to NPC Y, then kill Enemy Z.”
Instead, the game provides enough narrative direction to encourage exploration and discovery.
 
⸻
 
3. THREE STARTING STORYLINES
The game begins with three distinct starting locations and protagonists.
Each represents a different life and a different perspective on the same world.
The three storylines are:
START A — FOREST FARMING SETTLEMENT
The protagonist is a young person from a small farming settlement situated within/near an ancient forest.
They join the King’s Army seeking adventure and an escape from their ordinary life.
Their story therefore naturally exposes the player to:
rural life
the forest
the King’s Army
local dangers
monsters in the wilderness
the kingdom’s military situation
the growing wider crisis
 
⸻
 
START B — COASTAL TRADING TOWN
The protagonist is an orphan street rat living in a coastal trading town.
After saving a merchant from bandits, they earn a place within the merchant’s household.
Their story naturally exposes the player to:
coastal trade
merchants
commerce
crime
bandits
smuggling
pirates
the disruption of trade
the economic consequences of events elsewhere in the world
 
⸻
 
START C — HUMAN MOUNTAIN SETTLEMENT
The protagonist is a blacksmith apprentice living in a human settlement in the mountains.
Their normal life involves travelling to and from a nearby dwarven city:
delivering tools
returning with ore
Their story therefore naturally exposes the player to:
mountain settlements
blacksmithing
dwarven society
mining
mountain passes
trade between humans and dwarves
the increasing danger emerging from the deep mountains
 
⸻
 
4. THE THREE STORIES ARE CANON
The three starting stories are not alternate universes.
They are not:
“Choose one campaign and ignore the other two.”
All three protagonists exist within the same world and the same broader timeline.
Each storyline shows a different portion of the same historical events.
Therefore:
Forest Story
     \
      \
       → Shared World History
      /
     /
Coastal Story

Mountain Story
The player experiences one protagonist’s perspective, but the world contains the consequences and evidence of events occurring beyond that protagonist’s immediate surroundings.
 
⸻
 
5. DIFFERENT PERSPECTIVES ON SHARED EVENTS
A central narrative feature is that major world events can be encountered from different perspectives.
For example, an event affecting trade may initially appear to the coastal protagonist as:
Ships are disappearing. Prices are rising. Merchants are becoming nervous.
The mountain protagonist may experience the same wider crisis through:
Dangerous passes. Reduced trade. Fewer mining supplies. Dwarven production being disrupted.
The forest/army protagonist may experience it through:
Military mobilisation. Bandit activity. Refugees. Reports of trouble elsewhere.
These are not contradictory stories.
They are different pieces of the same world event.
 
⸻
 
6. SHARED TIMELINE
The three storylines exist on a common timeline.
Events happening in one region can have consequences elsewhere.
The world therefore has:
Persistent shared history.
The player should gradually realise that seemingly unrelated problems are connected.
 
⸻
 
7. NARRATIVE REVELATION
The game should encourage the player to construct an understanding of the world from fragments.
The player may first encounter:
Local problem
and later discover:
Regional consequence
and eventually understand:
Part of a much larger event
This creates narrative depth without requiring conventional exposition dumps.
 
⸻
 
8. THE JOURNAL
The primary narrative delivery mechanism is the Journal.
The Journal records story information as the player discovers it.
It should become a growing record of the player’s understanding of the world.
The Journal can contain:
story bites
discoveries
important people
locations
events
lore
rumours
major historical information
quest context
significant observations
 
⸻
 
9. JOURNAL STORY BITES
A story bite is a short piece of narrative content.
Story bites should generally be:
brief
readable
atmospheric
information-rich
attached to something the player has encountered
The game should favour:
Many small discoveries
over:
Large blocks of exposition.
 
⸻
 
10. STORY BITE EXAMPLE STRUCTURE
Conceptually:
JOURNAL — THE MOUNTAIN PASS

The dwarves have stopped sending ore south.

At first the merchants blamed the winter.
Then the miners began speaking of things
moving beneath the old tunnels.

The pass has now been closed.
The player is given enough information to understand the significance without receiving an entire chapter of exposition.
 
⸻
 
11. STORY BITES ARE WORLD-ATTACHED
Story bites should frequently originate from actual gameplay.
A story bite can be triggered by:
discovering a location
meeting an NPC
visiting a point of interest
encountering an enemy
entering a region
completing a journey
completing a quest
discovering an object
witnessing a story event
defeating a significant enemy
The narrative therefore feels embedded in the world.
 
⸻
 
12. POINTS OF INTEREST
Points of Interest are important exploration nodes.
A POI can contain:
a story bite
lore
an item
an encounter
an NPC
a quest
a discovery
environmental information
a clue to a larger event
POIs allow the player to investigate the world without requiring every piece of information to be part of a main quest.
 
⸻
 
13. LOCATION STORY
Locations themselves should have narrative identity.
A settlement is not simply:
“A shop + quest board + safe area.”
It should tell the player something about the world.
For example:
architecture
people
trade
local dangers
rumours
political circumstances
recent events
can all contribute to its story.
 
⸻
 
14. WORLD DESIGN
The setting is intentionally relatively ordinary low-magic fantasy.
It should feel grounded rather than like a world where every person is a wizard and magic solves every problem.
The world contains familiar fantasy societies and environments, but the deeper history contains mysteries that are far stranger than the ordinary present-day world.
 
⸻
 
15. THE PRESENT-DAY WORLD
The established world contains:
temperate kingdoms
ancient forests
eleven settlements
high mountain regions
dwarven cities
deserts
oases
coastal towns
pirates
smugglers
marshes and swamps
ancient ruins
haunted battlefields
political intrigue
trade guilds
adventurer guilds
monster-infested wilderness
These regions should feel geographically and economically connected.
 
⸻
 
16. ELEVEN SETTLEMENTS
The world contains eleven significant settlements.
They should form a connected network rather than eleven interchangeable towns.
Their identities emerge from their:
geography
economy
population
political position
nearby dangers
trade relationships
history
The three starting settlements are particularly important because they establish the three initial perspectives.
The remaining settlements become part of the player’s expanding understanding of the shared world.
 
⸻
 
17. FORESTS
The ancient forests are among the major wilderness environments.
They provide:
dangerous wilderness
isolated settlements
monsters
exploration
old mysteries
potential ruins
opportunities for the forest protagonist’s storyline
The forests should feel ancient and somewhat unknowable without becoming a stereotypical high-magic enchanted forest.
 
⸻
 
18. MOUNTAINS
The high mountains contain:
human settlements
dwarven cities
mining operations
dangerous passes
deep underground regions
monsters emerging from below
The mountains are particularly important to the world’s present crisis.
 
⸻
 
19. THE DEEP-MOUNTAIN THREAT
Monsters are emerging from the deep mountains.
This is not simply a local monster problem.
The consequences include:
dangerous mountain passes
disrupted trade
reduced access to dwarven cities
disrupted dwarven mining
reduced ore movement
wider economic consequences
This is one of the major shared-world pressures.
 
⸻
 
20. THE COAST AND TRADE
The coastal regions are important centres of commerce.
The world depends on trade between settlements and regions.
As land routes become increasingly dangerous, maritime trade becomes more important.
This creates opportunities for:
merchants
sailors
pirates
smugglers
coastal crime
economic intrigue
 
⸻
 
21. PIRACY AND SMUGGLING
Piracy and smuggling are not isolated adventure tropes.
They emerge partly from the disruption of legitimate trade.
When safer/shorter trade routes become unavailable, merchants are forced onto longer and more dangerous sea routes.
This creates conditions for:
piracy
smuggling
price increases
shortages
criminal networks
political tension
The coastal protagonist experiences these consequences directly.
 
⸻
 
22. THE DESERT
The desert contains tribes with an unusual degree of unity.
Normally disparate groups do not commonly act as a single political force.
In the current period, however, the desert tribes have united sufficiently to conduct raids against distant settlements.
This creates a major geopolitical pressure far beyond the desert itself.
 
⸻
 
23. MARSHES AND SWAMPS
Marsh and swamp regions form another distinct wilderness environment.
They can contain:
settlements
dangerous wildlife
monsters
ruins
isolated communities
hidden routes
story discoveries
They should provide a different exploration identity from forests, mountains and deserts.
 
⸻
 
24. HAUNTED BATTLEFIELDS
The world contains battlefields with lingering supernatural significance.
These locations provide an opportunity to show that although great magic has largely disappeared, the world still contains remnants of things that ordinary people do not fully understand.
Haunted battlefields can contain:
historical discoveries
ghosts/undead
old weapons
lost stories
journal discoveries
connections to previous conflicts
 
⸻
 
25. ANCIENT RUINS
Ancient ruins are remnants of a much older civilisation.
They should initially appear as worn, mysterious remnants rather than functioning magical cities.
Their importance increases as the player learns more about the world’s history.
 
⸻
 
26. THE ANCIENT HUMAN CIVILISATION
In the distant past, humanity possessed a civilisation capable of harnessing extremely deep forms of magic.
This civilisation was substantially more advanced than the present world.
It possessed:
powerful magical knowledge
mechanisms
lost technologies/magical systems
capabilities no longer understood by modern societies
Most of this knowledge is gone.
 
⸻
 
27. THE PORTAL EVENT
The ancient civilisation attempted to create or use a portal to another realm.
The attempt catastrophically destroyed the civilisation.
The event resulted in the loss of:
knowledge
mechanisms
magical understanding
much of the civilisation itself
Only extremely worn ruins remain.
This is one of the deeper mysteries beneath the otherwise ordinary fantasy setting.
 
⸻
 
28. LOST MAGIC
The world’s present inhabitants do not possess the magic of the ancient civilisation.
Great magic is:
Gone, scarce or poorly understood.
This is intentional.
The game should not drift into a high-magic setting simply because ancient magical history exists.
The ancient civilisation represents something largely lost.
 
⸻
 
29. PRIESTS AND RELIGION
Religion exists but is comparatively uncommon.
Priests are rare.
However, genuinely powerful priests exist.
Their abilities should feel significant precisely because they are unusual.
Religion can therefore contribute to:
mystery
healing
supernatural events
historical knowledge
political influence
story discoveries
without making magic commonplace.
 
⸻
 
30. WIZARDS AND WITCHES
Powerful wizards and witches still exist but tend to be:
reclusive
isolated
suspicious of strangers
difficult to approach
They should not function as ordinary shopkeepers or ubiquitous quest-givers.
Their rarity reinforces the world’s low-magic character.
 
⸻
 
31. KINGS AND CHIEFTAINS
The world contains powerful political leaders.
These include:
kings
nobles
warlike chieftains
Political power is an important source of conflict.
Not every threat is supernatural.
 
⸻
 
32. THE KING
The player’s forest-origin protagonist eventually enters the orbit of a king who is broadly regarded as wise and is supported by the kingdom.
However, the kingdom is not politically stable.
The king faces:
noble intrigue
civil unrest
crime
banditry
external threats
monster threats
This creates a grounded political layer beneath the larger supernatural mystery.
 
⸻
 
33. POLITICAL INTRIGUE
Political conflict should operate alongside the monster and exploration stories.
The world is not divided into:
Good kingdom vs evil kingdom.
Instead, characters can have competing interests.
Political story material can involve:
nobles
merchants
military interests
local leaders
guilds
criminals
foreign powers
desert tribes
dwarven interests
 
⸻
 
34. GUILDS
The world contains established trade and adventurer guild structures.
Guilds provide a natural connection between:
exploration
commerce
quests
adventuring
information
rewards
They can also provide a grounded way for the protagonist to become involved in increasingly important events.
 
⸻
 
35. MONSTER WILDERNESS
The wilderness is dangerous.
Monsters are not restricted to scripted boss arenas.
They inhabit the world and make travel genuinely dangerous.
This supports the fundamental gameplay rule:
Unsafe-area travel requires combat.
 
⸻
 
36. CENTRAL REGIONS
Not every part of the world is equally dangerous.
The central regions remain comparatively safe.
This creates a gradient:
Civilised / central areas
        ↓
Frontier
        ↓
Dangerous wilderness
        ↓
Remote / hostile regions
The player therefore has a recognisable progression from safety into danger.
 
⸻
 
37. STORY AND EXPLORATION
The player receives a broad journal objective rather than a rigid navigation checklist.
For example, the Journal might establish:
“Find out why the dwarves have stopped sending ore.”
It should not necessarily say:
“Walk to X, then talk to Y, then travel to Z.”
The player discovers the path through:
exploration
NPCs
locations
POIs
journeys
encounters
journal story bites
 
⸻
 
38. REQUIRED STORY VS FLAVOUR
There is an explicit distinction between:
Required Story
Major narrative events required for progression.
These cannot be permanently missed.
Flavour / Side Content
Optional discoveries that deepen the world.
These remain available after their region/content becomes unlocked and do not block main-story progression.
 
⸻
 
39. REQUIRED STORY DISCOVERY
Required story content can still be discovered naturally.
The player should not be able to accidentally destroy their campaign by overlooking a particular NPC or POI.
The system can therefore ensure that required story beats eventually become available through the appropriate progression path.
 
⸻
 
40. OPTIONAL DISCOVERY
Optional content can remain permanently available once its relevant region/content has been unlocked.
Examples include:
additional journal entries
minor NPC stories
historical discoveries
optional POIs
lore
optional combat
side quests
The player can return to this content without jeopardising the main narrative.
 
⸻
 
41. NO DIALOGUE-CHOICE PROTAGONIST
The three protagonists are intentionally designed as blank observers rather than strongly authored RPG personalities.
The player provides the protagonist’s name.
The protagonist does not have a fixed:
gender
personality
detailed biography
dialogue-choice personality
The character’s starting circumstances and broad life experience are fixed by the selected starting storyline.
 
⸻
 
42. NPC INTERACTIONS
NPC interactions are presented or summarised rather than being built around extensive player dialogue trees.
The player is not repeatedly asked:
“What personality response should your character give?”
The protagonist’s role is primarily to experience and observe the world.
This keeps the narrative focused on:
world discovery
events
consequences
characters
the protagonist’s journey
rather than branching conversational role-play.
 
⸻
 
43. NARRATIVE AGENCY
Player agency is expressed primarily through:
exploration
route selection
build choices
equipment
optional content
journey selection
progression
rather than through dozens of dialogue branches.
 
⸻

45. CROSS-STORY RECOGNITION
As the wider story develops, the player should encounter evidence that events they’ve heard about locally are occurring elsewhere.
A story bite may therefore reference:
a distant war
missing shipments
refugees
monster attacks
political developments
unusual weather/events
changes in trade
The player may understand the significance differently depending on their starting storyline.
 
⸻
 
46. NARRATIVE PAYOFF
Later story events should reward the player’s accumulated understanding.
Information encountered as isolated journal bites should eventually connect.
The intended experience is:
Small clue
   ↓
Another clue
   ↓
Apparently unrelated event
   ↓
Pattern recognised
   ↓
Historical connection
   ↓
Major revelation
This is particularly important for the ancient civilisation and portal mystery.
 
⸻
 
47. ANCIENT MYSTERY STRUCTURE
The ancient civilisation should not be explained immediately.
Its story should be discovered gradually through:
ruins
artefacts
journal entries
NPC knowledge
unusual mechanisms
historical records
supernatural encounters
The player should first encounter fragments before understanding the larger truth.
 
⸻
 
48. PRESENT VS ANCIENT STORY
The narrative operates on two broad layers:
Present-Day Crisis
monsters
trade disruption
piracy
desert raids
political intrigue
crime
civil unrest
dangerous wilderness
Ancient Mystery
lost civilisation
deep magic
lost mechanisms
portal attempt
destruction of the civilisation
remnants in ruins
The story gradually reveals whether and how these layers are connected.
 
⸻
 
49. NARRATIVE ESCALATION
The story should begin grounded.
The protagonist initially has relatively ordinary concerns appropriate to their starting location.
The scale then expands:
Personal Life
    ↓
Local Problems
    ↓
Regional Problems
    ↓
Kingdom / World Problems
    ↓
Ancient Mystery
    ↓
Larger Truth
The player should not begin with complete knowledge of the world’s deepest mystery.
 
⸻
 
50. STORY AND COMBAT
Combat is an integrated narrative mechanic.
Combat is required for:
travelling through unsafe areas
overcoming story obstacles
defeating story bosses
Optional combat remains available for:
XP
weapon skill
loot
gold
Combat therefore serves both narrative progression and character progression.
 
⸻
 
51. STORY AND JOURNEYS
Journeys provide the physical structure through which much of the story unfolds.
A story journey may contain:
Travel
 ↓
Encounter
 ↓
Journal Story Bite
 ↓
Point of Interest
 ↓
Combat
 ↓
NPC/Event
 ↓
Story Discovery
 ↓
Boss
 ↓
Narrative Resolution
The Journey system controls movement and encounter resolution.
The Story system controls narrative content.
 
⸻
 
52. STORY AND DUNGEONS
Dungeons can contain important narrative discoveries.
They can reveal:
lost history
factions
monsters
ancient technology
Story Relics
historical events
major story information
Dungeons therefore serve both gameplay and narrative purposes.
 
⸻
 
53. STORY RELICS
Story Relics are narrative-significant equipment.
The player has a dedicated:
Story Relic equipment slot.
Story Relics are obtained through story progression or other authored narrative content.
They can possess meaningful gameplay effects.
 
⸻
 
54. STORY RELIC PROTECTION
Story Relics are never lost through ordinary defeat.
This is both a gameplay and narrative requirement.
The player cannot lose an important piece of narrative progression because they failed a journey.

A single world event can therefore produce different story bites depending on where/how the player encounters it.

59. SHARED WORLD EVENT PRINCIPLE
The same world event should not need to be duplicated as three completely separate pieces of story logic.
Instead:
             WORLD EVENT
                  │
       ┌──────────┼──────────┐
       ↓          ↓          ↓
   Forest      Coastal    Mountain
 Perspective  Perspective Perspective
       │          │          │
       └──────────┼──────────┘
                  ↓
          Shared Story State
This keeps the narrative coherent while preserving the uniqueness of each starting storyline.
